On the Governance of the River Province

The River Province has long been administered under a single charter, and every
office within it draws its authority from that founding document. The charter is
old, but its provisions remain in force, and every magistrate swears to uphold
them upon taking office. This is the first principle of the province.

Every magistrate in the River Province is an officer of the charter. Every
officer of the charter is bound by the oath of service. Every person bound by
the oath of service must publish their decisions. These three rules form the
backbone of provincial administration, and they have not been amended in living
memory.

The province is divided into seven districts, each with its own assembly. Each
assembly elects a magistrate, and each magistrate appoints a clerk. The clerks
keep the records, and the records are stored in the provincial archive. The
archive is open to any citizen who requests access, subject to the usual hours.

Now consider the district of Lower Marsh. The assembly of Lower Marsh elected a
magistrate named Corin in the spring. Corin is therefore a magistrate in the
River Province. By the rules already stated, Corin is an officer of the charter,
Corin is bound by the oath of service, and Corin must publish their decisions.

Corin appointed a clerk named Tamsin shortly after taking office. Tamsin keeps
the records of Lower Marsh, and those records are stored in the provincial
archive like all the others. Any citizen may request to see them during the
usual hours, for that is the standing rule of the archive.

The province also maintains a council of elders, separate from the assemblies.
The council advises the magistrates but does not command them. Every elder is a
citizen of long standing, and every elder has served at least one term in an
assembly. The council meets twice a year to review the state of the province.

Some citizens of the River Province are merchants, and some merchants trade
beyond the provincial borders. Trade beyond the borders is taxed at the river
gate, and the gate-keepers record every consignment. The records of the gate are
also stored in the archive, alongside the district records.

In all these arrangements the province has remained orderly. The charter is
upheld, the magistrates publish their decisions, the clerks keep the records,
and the archive remains open. Corin and Tamsin carry out their duties as the
rules require, and Lower Marsh is governed no differently from the other six
districts.
